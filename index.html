Mission 1

Alpha is a hyperparameter, not just a hack

Last time, $\alpha = 1$ was picked almost as an afterthought — just enough to avoid dividing by zero. Here's what happens at the extremes, and why alpha deserves to be tuned just as carefully as $k$ in kNN.

Say the training data has $n_1 = 100$ spam messages, and a word $w_1$ occurs in $n_{j1} = 10$ of them. Drag the slider or use a preset to see the smoothed likelihood shift:

0α = 010,000
P(w₁ | y=1) = (10 + 0) / (100 + 2×0) = 0.1000
0.00.5 (pure guess)1.0
Think it through As alpha grows huge, do you expect every word's likelihood to drift toward the same value, or stay spread out based on how often each word actually appeared?
They all drift toward the same value: $1/C = 0.5$. When $\alpha$ is far larger than the real count $n_{j1}$, that count becomes irrelevant in the fraction, and the ratio collapses toward $\frac{\alpha}{C\alpha} = 0.5$ — for every word equally, regardless of how predictive it actually was.

What happens to the final prediction?

If every likelihood term is roughly 0.5, the product of likelihoods for both classes becomes roughly equal, and cancels out when comparing the two classes. The prediction ends up depending only on the class priors.

Suppose, hypothetically, $P(y=1) = 0.7$ and $P(y=0) = 0.3$. With likelihoods cancelled out, Naive Bayes would predict spam for every single message, regardless of its actual words. The model has stopped looking at the text entirely.

This is underfittingAlpha too large means the model ignores the data (the likelihoods) and falls back on the priors alone.

And a very small alpha?

As $\alpha \to 0$, the smoothed likelihood converges back to the unsmoothed value, $\frac{n_{j1}}{n_1}$. This fits the training data's exact word frequencies as closely as possible, including any noise from words that appeared rarely by chance. This is overfitting.

At test time, a genuinely new word that's part of the vocabulary stays protected by any $\alpha$ strictly greater than zero — that's the zero-probability problem from last time, distinct from the underflow problem coming up next. As $\alpha \to 0$ that protection gets vanishingly weak, even though it's technically still nonzero.

Summary: alpha genuinely controls the bias-variance tradeoff, exactly like $k$ in kNN. Larger alpha means more underfitting; smaller alpha means more overfitting.

Mission 2

What imbalance does to Naive Bayes

MailNest's inbox skews heavily ham over spam. Here's exactly how that imbalance can distort a prediction — in the direction that matters most for a spam filter: letting real spam slip through disguised as ham.

Suppose $P(y=0)=0.7$ and $P(y=1)=0.3$ (a ham-heavy prior, matching the real data). For a query message, the combined likelihoods work out to $P(\text{words}\mid y=1)=0.2$ and $P(\text{words}\mid y=0)=0.1$ — the words themselves actually point toward spam.

Rebalance the classes (equalize priors)

SPAM SCORE — P(y=1) × P(words|y=1)

0.3 × 0.2 = 0.060

HAM SCORE — P(y=0) × P(words|y=0)

0.7 × 0.1 = 0.070
Predicted: HAM — even though the word evidence favored spam (0.2 > 0.1), the ham-heavy prior overrides it.

Rebalancing the training data — for example oversampling the minority spam class until both classes are equally represented — sets $P(y=1)=P(y=0)=0.5$. With equal priors, they cancel out when comparing classes, leaving the likelihoods to genuinely drive the prediction. (This is distinct from SMOTE, which generates synthetic minority points by interpolation rather than duplicating rows outright — both rebalance the priors the same way.)

Does rebalancing distort the likelihoods themselves?

No. Suppose there are $n_0=1000$ ham messages and $n_1=500$ spam messages, and a word $w_j$ occurs in $n_{j1}=50$ of the spam messages.

Before

P(wⱼ | y=1) = 50 / 500

= 0.1

After duplicating to n₁=1000

P(wⱼ | y=1) = 100 / 1000

= 0.1 — unchanged

Rebalancing only touches the class priors; it leaves the word-level likelihoods exactly as they were.

Mission 3

The underflow problem

Naive Bayes multiplies together one class prior and $d$ likelihood values, all between 0 and 1. Imagine 2,000 words in a long message, each likelihood a plausible value between 0.05 and 0.3 — here's what happens to the running product as more words are multiplied in:

Bars show −log₁₀(product) — taller means a smaller (closer-to-zero) product. The red striped bars are where the true value is nonzero but too small for a computer to represent: it gets rounded down to exactly 0.0.

By a few hundred words in, the product has already collapsed to 0.0, even though every individual likelihood was perfectly reasonable. A computer stores numbers with a fixed number of bits (commonly float64), and a result this small simply can't be represented. This is the underflow problem, and it silently breaks every comparison between $P(y=1\mid\text{text})$ and $P(y=0\mid\text{text})$ once both collapse to zero.

Using fewer words avoids the problem, but throws away real information — exactly the words that might have been most useful for a long, word-rich message. That's not the fix.

The real fix: work in log-space

$$P(y=1 \mid w_1, \ldots, w_d) = P(y=1) \prod_{j=1}^{d} P(w_j \mid y=1)$$

Take the log of both sides. Since $\log(ab) = \log a + \log b$, the product turns into a sum:

Log-space posterior$$\log P(y=1 \mid w_1, \ldots, w_d) = \log P(y=1) + \sum_{j=1}^{d} \log P(w_j \mid y=1)$$

Summing many small numbers together is numerically stable in a way multiplying them never is — the numbers stay in a normal range instead of shrinking toward zero.

Sum of logs (stable): −3696.62
Exponentiating back: 0.0 ← still underflows, but we never needed this value directly

Is comparing logs still valid?

Yes. Log is monotonic: if $a > b$, then $\log a > \log b$, always — regardless of whether the results are negative, which they will be here since we're taking the log of numbers less than 1. So comparing $\log P(y=1\mid\text{text})$ against $\log P(y=0\mid\text{text})$ gives exactly the same winner the raw probabilities would have, without ever computing those tiny raw values at all.

If the actual probability value is needed back, not just the winning class, naively exponentiating the summed log still underflows. The standard safe way to recover it is the log-sum-exp trick.

Mission 4

Reading feature importance straight from the model

A trained model's "weights" are exactly its class priors and likelihoods. The priors don't depend on which words are in any particular message, so they can't explain why this specific message got its prediction. For that, we need the likelihoods.

It's tempting to look at $P(w_j \mid \hat{y})$ — the raw likelihood under the predicted class — and call the largest values "most important." This is misleading: a word can have a large likelihood under spam and still tell us almost nothing, if its likelihood under ham is just as large.

The right measure: likelihood ratio$$\text{ratio}_j = \frac{P(w_j \mid y=1)}{P(w_j \mid y=0)}$$

A ratio near 1 means the word barely discriminates, no matter how large either likelihood is. A ratio far from 1 means the word genuinely pulls the prediction one way.

A query message contains "call," "u," "free," and "prize," and Naive Bayes predicted spam:

WordP(w|y=1)P(w|y=0)Ratio

This is exactly the explainability MailNest's Trust & Safety team asked for: for any flagged message, point to the words with the most extreme likelihood ratios, not the largest raw likelihoods, as the ones that tipped the decision.

Mission 5

Outliers at training time and at test time

Test-time outlier

A word Laplace smoothing protects

Part of the vocabulary (seen in training), but zero occurrences in one class. Smoothing keeps its likelihood strictly positive instead of exactly zero.

A sharper gap

A word smoothing can't touch

A real CountVectorizer pipeline builds its vocabulary only from training data. A test word never seen anywhere in training isn't a column at all — it's silently dropped, not assigned a zero. Smoothing has nothing to act on.

If MailNest wants to catch brand-new scam vocabulary the moment it appears, this gap — not smoothing — is where to invest next: retraining on a rolling window of recent messages, or falling back to character-level or subword features that can say something about a word never seen whole.

Train-time outliers

A different kind of outlier hides inside the training data itself: a word that appears extremely rarely. Suppose a word $w_j$ occurs in only $n_{j1}=1$ spam message out of $n_1=1000$ total:

$$P(w_j \mid y=1) = \frac{1}{1000} = 0.001$$

This word might be genuinely rare, or it might just be noise — a typo, an unusual name — that happened to appear once. A likelihood this small, this confidently, based on a single occurrence, is risky to trust.

Preferred

A slightly larger alpha

Pulls the single-occurrence likelihood toward a more moderate value, without going anywhere near Mission 1's underfitting territory.

Blunter

A minimum-count threshold

Only include a word if it occurs more than some threshold (say, 10+ times). All-or-nothing — a word either counts fully or is ignored entirely.

Smoothing handles both rare training words and entirely unseen test words more gracefully, using the same mechanism. One practical constraint: with $d$ potentially in the thousands, tuning a separate alpha per word is impractical — a single shared alpha across all words is what's actually used.

Mission 6

Bernoulli vs. Multinomial Naive Bayes

Everything so far has quietly assumed each word is either present or absent in a message — a 0/1 feature. This is Bernoulli Naive Bayes. "Free" appearing once might be a mild signal; "free free free free!!!" appearing four times is a much stronger one — Bernoulli throws that difference away entirely.

Multinomial Naive Bayes instead stores the actual count of each word per message.

Mission 7

Building the real filter

CountVectorizer converts every message into a row of word counts, one column per unique word across the whole training set. Any single message only contains a handful of them, so most entries are 0 — the result is stored as a sparse matrix, keeping only the non-zero counts and saving enormous memory.

4,179 × 7,615
training messages × vocabulary
1,393 × 7,615
test messages × same vocabulary
Fit only on training data. Exactly like a scaler, CountVectorizer is fit once on training messages, then used to transform the test messages — never re-fit on test data. Because the vocabulary is fixed at fit time, any word in a test message that never appeared in training is silently dropped when transforming. That's the gap Mission 5 flagged: smoothing rescues in-vocabulary words that are merely rare in one class, but can't rescue a word the vectorizer never learned about at all.

Does Naive Bayes need scaled features? No. Unlike kNN, which is distance-based, Naive Bayes is an information-based algorithm — it works with probabilities and counts, not distances between points. Scaling exists to make distances comparable across features; it has no role here.

Tuning alpha properly

Mission 1 showed alpha genuinely trades off under- and overfitting — time to tune it with cross-validation, the same way max_depth was tuned earlier in the course. Because the data is imbalanced, F1 score (not accuracy) is used for scoring: it balances precision and recall, giving a far more honest picture than accuracy would on a lopsided dataset.

Wrap-up

What we now know

  • Alpha is a real hyperparameter, not just a zero-avoidance hack: too large and the likelihoods collapse toward 0.5, leaving predictions dependent only on the class priors (underfitting); too small and the model fits training noise too closely (overfitting).
  • Class imbalance can distort predictions by letting the priors dominate over the likelihoods. Rebalancing fixes this by equalising the priors, and provably leaves the likelihoods themselves unchanged.
  • Multiplying many probabilities together causes underflow. Working in log-space turns that product into a sum, which stays numerically stable, and comparisons between classes remain valid since log is monotonic.
  • A trained model's likelihoods double as feature importance: the words with the largest likelihood ratio for the predicted class are the ones actually driving that prediction — not the largest raw likelihood.
  • Test-time outliers are unseen words, handled by Laplace smoothing. Train-time outliers are rare words with very few occurrences, best handled with a slightly larger alpha rather than a hard count threshold.
  • Bernoulli Naive Bayes tracks word presence/absence; Multinomial Naive Bayes tracks word counts, capturing repetition at a higher computational cost.
  • CountVectorizer turns text into a sparse count matrix, fit on training data only. Naive Bayes needs no feature scaling, since it's information-based, not distance-based.
